## I. The novel beneath the farming game

**Player-facing premise:** A person arrives in early autumn to work a neglected farmhouse above a village where fog has settled into daily life. The property offers shelter, a livelihood and a chance to begin differently. It also comes with people, records and unresolved obligations the farmer did not create. What remains of Agnes and Elias Rook is not the player's biography. It is a history the player may choose to approach, while their own history continues to ask for attention.

**The central contradiction:** Having a place to live is not the same as owning it; owning a place is not the same as belonging there; belonging is not proof that someone owes everything to everyone else.

**Year One dramatic question:** Can I make a life here?  
**Year Two dramatic question:** What does this place require of me now that I have one?  
**Personal parallel question:** What happens to the life I left when I am no longer there to maintain it?

All three questions must remain valid for a self-sufficient farmer who rarely visits town, an active trader, a quiet builder, a generous neighbor or a curious investigator. There is no global virtue score or assumed model citizen.

### Narrative promise to the player
1. Your arrival was chosen for a reason; it is neither a cosmetic personality test nor a compulsory personal tragedy.
2. All five origins receive a beginning, an external winter development, at least one material spring/summer decision and a year-end/second-year consequence.
3. What you do is remembered more reliably than how a narrator assumes you feel. Relationships respond to available knowledge, not omniscience.
4. Work matters materially. A plank becomes a repair; a harvest becomes income, a meal or a request fulfilled.
5. Rural life must be interesting without the supernatural mystery. Extraordinary events are powerful because ordinary life continues around them.
6. The original 17-chapter story and its three authored endings are not bypassed by friendliness, hidden dice, family background or town reputation.
7. Endings resolve the existing Hollow event, not the farmer's entire life; ordinary work and some unanswered human questions remain.

### Voice and scene craft
Use restrained, specific, plain language. A line about a wet envelope beside the dishes carries more force than a paragraph naming grief, loneliness or fate. Dialogue emerges from tasks and particular people; avoid exposition monologues and repeated hints that everyone secretly knows something. Characters can be funny, contradictory, mistaken and occupied. Give them dignity when the player declines help. Let scenes end before they explain themselves.

**Five recurring material motifs** (from the Literary Structure source): wood (use and shelter); light (ordinary care, not moral purity); paper (fallible records); water (work, weather and displacement); bells and working sounds (the contrast between daily community life and rare silence). No object is required to turn magical. In particular, contradictory Wicker Cove fog testimony does not establish its supernatural source.

## III. Act Zero: The Road to Crow Creek Hollow

**Owner-required form:** Fully interactive, mandatory, non-skippable opening for every new game. Five route-specific lives, not a short optional montage. The player chooses their starting reason from a concrete scene with a farm notice; this changes the next illustrated/text scenes, departure action, carried objects/contact and later dialogue and correspondence. Access must include pause/resume, Back/Previous where appropriate, unhurried reading and no lost first-day clock time. The 7–10-minute screenplay target is an editorial estimate to playtest, not a demand for prolonged passive exposition.

**Original full authored scene source:** ROAD_TO_CROW_CREEK_HOLLOW_BRANCHING_SCREENPLAY_DRAFT.md. It contains Scene 00, five Act 1 branches, person/contact and keepsake decisions, road transitions and winter return scenes. This bible intentionally does not substitute abbreviated prose for that authored screenplay.

### The five origins are different *circumstances*, not class/stat choices
| Origin | Why leave | Personal question | Spring/summer stake | Explicitly NOT presumed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — The Cost of a Room | Old housing terms changed; farmland offers a practical alternative | What makes a place secure? | Settle stored belongings, forwarding logistics or old housing account | Poverty as a moral flaw; unavoidable eviction |
| B — Work That Belonged to Everyone | Want one's labor and time to be one's own | When do past obligations stop? | Offer limited paid help, answer once, or decline a former coworker's request | Fixed professional career or crafting bonus |
| C — A House With One Less Person | Household arrangement changed | What remains when familiar daily patterns change? | Decide what to do with a shared item, message or relationship boundary | Death, bereavement, divorce or romance |
| D — The Place in the Photograph | Curious about a real place and wants to go | Was taking the journey itself enough? | Decide how to represent the place to someone from before and whether to share details | Supernatural destiny or prophecy |
| E — A Chance Before It Closed | A contingent practical opportunity arose | How long does an old chance keep a claim on you? | Close, maintain or relinquish a prior opportunity, contract or practical obligation | An empty 'no backstory' origin |

### Core opening integrity
- No one is compelled to disclose romantic, family or medical history; undisclosed details cannot later be asserted.
- No origin begins with free title or secret biological relation to the Rooks.
- Past contacts have ordinary limits and agency, and letters need credible sender/forwarding routes.
- Physical choices form saved facts: what was kept, mailed, given away, promised and witnessed. Only later refer to those facts where truly known.
- A keepsake or letter should be modest: not a new sword, automatic stat boost or quest-replacing evidence.
- The same gate, first morning and existing tutorial await all origins.

**Suggested editorial through-line:** The opening ends not on an ominous revelation but on a practical act at the farmhouse threshold: entering, setting down a box, observing damaged boards, or checking that the door shuts. Its meaning can change later when the player reaches that door in different circumstances. No forced internal narration such as 'you knew you were home.'

## IV. The two-life braid: how origins continue

Five origin episodes share a structural pattern. They do not need five independently simulated cities, but they require five distinct *narrative situations and actual choices*.

### Autumn: The Stranger
The farmer unpacks something from the previous life, or handles a corresponding mundane piece of mail. An accepted contact might hear that the farmer arrived; a withheld-contact path stays private. The physical house is unfinished. The first wooden repair, first crop and first night carry the central theme through action rather than exposition. Old Maren's day-four edge journey must occur through its established route.

**Scene placement rule:** A low-pressure encounter after the player has actually learned to move, plant and sleep. Do not interrupt a time-sensitive survival day with a second mandatory cutscene after Act Zero.

### Winter: The Keeper
A consequence outside the player's control arrives from the former life. Housing or possessions change hands; work continues without the player; a changed household makes a practical request; someone asks what the photograph journey became; a chance approaches an expiry. The player can respond or defer, with a fair eventual effect and no personal condemnation.

**Original full winter writing:** the branching opening screenplay includes the five winter return scenes. Each must stay origin-conditional. No magical way for a former acquaintance to know private farm events.

### Spring / early summer: The Neighbor
Each origin asks for one consequential answer that the player can actually carry out using mail, a small sum, a day of labor, a delivered object or a boundary spoken clearly. The spring return screenplay contains original detailed paths:
- A: pay/extend/release two stored boxes, or settle a different old-account path if no boxes were stored.
- B: paid consultation with limits, a one-time answer or no return to the former work.
- C: continued contact, transfer or closure of an object/household obligation without declaring a compulsory breakup or death.
- D: answer what was worth the journey, selectively share a real farm discovery or preserve privacy without fabricating a revelation.
- E: confirm, renegotiate or let go of an ordinary earlier opportunity; avoid infinite paperwork and punitive traps.

Actual rewards and costs must be reviewed against the 420-second day and economy. Declining cannot quietly erase the entire origin arc; that too is a meaningful resolution. These are **editorial targets**, not live save flags.

### September: The One Who Has Been Here
The diary draws together only witnessed and recorded things: one prior-life matter, a homestead action, perhaps a resident choice and a story event. The first harvest's ordinary significance can coexist with an unresolved bill or old friendship. No essay tells the player they have healed or transformed. Different players receive different true sentences.

### Second autumn and afterword
A simple physical return demonstrates persistence: a stored box now near the workbench; no more invoices; a former coworker who has moved on; a changed address; a person who still writes occasionally; a photograph now showing a second place. Do not require the original Hollow ending to resolve an ordinary prior-life matter. After any of the three endings, farm life and the personal origin can continue if underlying state permits.

## VI. The community is made of people, not quest dispensers

### Existing four walkers (story critical)
**Old Maren:** practical neighbor, Ruth's sister, window lamp, hens, old vote and minute-book evidence. Let her notice work and weather; never replace her records with a new charisma check.  
**Davy Tull:** a boy who skips stones and wants to find Pip; there is a family and ordinary life around his frightening second-autumn turn. Preserve the unique timed encounter and possible cost.  
**The vicar:** the bell's removed clapper, chapel register, quiet remorse and daily duties. His ordinary food/service needs can coexist with a difficult past; no new NPC may deliver his evidence for him.  
**The postman:** carries mail, retains privacy and eventually reveals a particular handwriting connection through the existing story. Do not spoil the reveal via generic ledger text. His Lowmere references still need geographic continuity review.

### Candidate Wicker Cove residents (not owner-finalized)

## VII. The house as a recurring chapter, not a speaking character

The house is seen repeatedly under changing weather, player work and obligations. Its memory is conveyed by honest changes, not a magical narrator or rigid emotional label.

Seven repeated places connect narrative and mechanics:
1. **Bedroom:** the first bed, repairs, insulation and ordinary rest.
2. **Kitchen/table:** meals beside bills, letters, crop ledgers and eventual deed if acquired.
3. **Hearth:** shelter, firewood and practical limits in winter.
4. **Workshop:** raw logs become planks *when implemented*, then become repairs.
5. **Door/mailbox:** strangers, ordinary notices, the familiar postman, and later original knock.
6. **Market stand/fields:** work becomes a livelihood; sale is not lesser than gift.
7. **Fence/road/forest edge:** boundaries, return from labor, unsettling proximity without constant menace.

The actual current interior/art state must be checked before writing lines that mention restored curtains, furniture or stacked planks. Later restoration stages bring truly visible comfort; stage 0 still feels abandoned. The house's fixed footprint helps repeated spaces retain emotional continuity.

### One proposed recurring object technique (editorial, not new lore)
Choose an ordinary basket, envelope or board that can appear early, become involved in a real transaction, and later be visibly reused. The returning object's value derives from who used it and whether its state was saved—not mystical properties or a sentimental paragraph. Avoid inventing dozens of persistent prop IDs before testing one.

## VIII. What makes choices meaningful without combinatorial explosion

A true choice has:
- A **readable situation** and at least two viable actions (often three: help, trade, decline/defer).
- A **clear immediate difference** in time/resources/work, relationship or access.
- A **persistent witnessed fact** rather than a moral/personality score.
- A **later callback** that is different enough to be observable but doesn't fork all game systems.
- A **reasonable way forward** when the player chooses caution, solitude or nonparticipation.

Choice lines should express intention or action, not assign an identity. An occasional hidden check may create uncertainty in genuinely risky repair/navigation/record handling, with disclosed stakes and recoverable failure. Never roll to determine sincerity, affection, consent, evidence for canonical endings or the origin choice.

**Source truth matters more than prose elegance:** the diary may write 'I sent the letter' only if a send action was completed; 'the room is repaired' only if the repair was paid and recorded; 'I found the grove page' only if the canonical page was acquired. No diary assumes 'I finally felt at home', 'I forgave them', or 'I was frightened' without player-authored wording.

Communication is not telepathy. NPCs know through direct observation, a delivered letter, a public transaction, a trusted messenger or disclosed conversation; if none occurred they should not refer to the player's private origin.

## IX. Horror, patience and the first/last door

**Three narrative registers:**
- Warm and frequent: cooking, wet boots, a failed repair, seed buying, jokes, a good harvest, a room made usable.
- Uncertain and occasional: the road grows quiet, a letter conflicts with a memory, water behaves strangely, a whistle appears out of place.
- Severe and rare: original first-night scare, orchard rise, knock, meeting, Davy's night, bell, grove/ending.

Ordinary activity must remain worthwhile regardless of supernatural advancement. A player may work on a roof while the mystery waits; a player may chase evidence without being punished for not restoring the inn. Balanced darkness and lantern radius upgrades support this: light creates enough visibility to love the world before it creates tension.

### Literary loop: threshold → return
The first arrival sets a box on the threshold of a damaged farmhouse. Much later, after a story-critical night, the player crosses the *same* threshold. The door hasn't changed position. What has changed might be its boards, what is on the table, who writes, whether the bell is hanging, whether a dog is absent, or who is still alive. Write the final passage from actual state. **Do not make the closing sentence 'now you truly belong.'** Let evidence, action and silence give players ownership of that meaning.
