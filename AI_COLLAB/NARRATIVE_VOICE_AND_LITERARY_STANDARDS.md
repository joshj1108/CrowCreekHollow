# CCH-NARRATIVE-VOICE-001 — Literary Depth, Plain Language

**Owner-approved creative direction, 2026-11-14:** Crow Creek Hollow should aspire to the emotional depth, nuance and lasting impact of fine literary fiction, written in **accessible, everyday language**. A lived-in novel, not ornate fantasy prose. Status: **editorial design standard**, not authorization to rewrite canon dialogue or add quests.

## North star
**Literary depth. Everyday language. Emotional weight that feels earned.** The game is a story about work, fear, family, obligation, community and making a home. Let ordinary details hold meaning. Mystery does not require obscure phrasing; strong characters do not speak in nonstop riddles. Leave room for readers/players to infer, disagree and reflect.

## Eight practical rules
1. **Use plain, specific nouns and verbs.** 'She set the wet boots beside the stove' usually beats 'Her weary form sought solace from the tempest.'
2. **Respect subtext.** A pause, an empty place at a table, work interrupted, a mistaken name or a bill saved for decades can communicate an emotion without naming it.
3. **Make voices distinct through priorities and rhythm, not caricature.** Maren is practical and guarded; Davy curious but observant; existing vicar burdened; postman experienced and matter-of-fact. Do not flatten them into exposition machines. Candidate Wicker Cove residents should have distinct work-shaped language, not identical rustic speech.
4. **Keep most dialogue short.** A line can do narrative work without a monologue. Let an occasional longer scene earn its length, particularly around the existing story beats.
5. **Let player action complete the sentence.** Instead of stating 'you are brave,' depict that they went outside when frightened. Never attribute regret, love, greed, or noble intention they never chose.
6. **Use recurring concrete motifs.** An unlit window, damp wood, quiet birds, an old bill, a dry lantern, a stool by the door, wet boots, a neatly repaired shelf. Return to an object *after the player changes its context*.
7. **Avoid over-explaining horror.** The extraordinary should interrupt ordinary activities. Don't label the fog's cause, true date of onset or relationship to the Hollow; residents and records disagree on its history.
8. **Choices should create complexity without a 'correct' authorial judgment.** Helping, bargaining and keeping resources can each be honest responses. Local consequences can still be difficult and materially unequal.

## Voice and point of view
- **Environmental narration:** short, observant, restrained; mostly concrete senses—sound, touch, light, smell, work. Never claim what the player feels if unchosen.
- **Dialogue:** spoken language with pauses and personality. Avoid faux-archaic dialect, overdone apostrophes, poetic speeches and modern meme language.
- **Player diary:** first-person, plain and reflective, but generated *only from verified facts and expressed interpretations*. First-person entry can describe what player did and saw; emotions require an authored attitude the player selected or an unambiguous on-screen action.
- **Agnes's diary:** preserve existing handwriting/voice and authored lore; any new text must be checked against original Agnes pages and chronology. Agnes may be more intimate or lyrical than player-facing UI, but not unintelligible.
- **UI/quest instructions:** direct and brief; clarify actual task. Don't hide controls/progression behind atmosphere.
- **Letters and books:** diction can reveal age, education, profession and motive. A contradictory harbor log should be readable as a believable work record, not a puzzle written by an omniscient narrator.
- **Marketing copy:** poetic compression is fine, but should remain accessible and not promise mechanics that aren't shipping.

## Contrast: overly written vs intended tone (original examples)

**Avoid:** 'The oppressive darkness filled the farmer with existential dread as the ancient wilderness revealed its malevolent presence.'

**Prefer:** 'The woods had been quiet all evening. Then the birds stopped. Something stood where the trees began.'

**Avoid:** 'The indomitable spirit of a proud coastal people struggled against an unknowable shroud of despair.'

**Prefer:** 'There were six boats at the dock. Only two looked ready to leave. Someone had hung clean nets beside the broken ones.'

**Avoid:** 'You regretted keeping the food while your neighbors went hungry.'

**Prefer (only if the player kept their supplies):** 'I kept what I had grown. The pantry lasted until the thaw. I heard the inn found another supplier.'

## First approach to Wicker Cove — voice sample (candidate scene, not canon dialogue)
> The road had been descending for nearly an hour.
>
> You smelled the sea before you saw the first house.
>
> Wicker Cove was smaller than you expected. A row of old buildings stood along the harbor road, their windows glowing yellow through the fog.
>
> Somewhere ahead, a man was hammering something back together.
>
> Two gulls argued over a scrap of fish.
>
> For a place that looked half abandoned, there was still a surprising amount of life.
>
> An older woman stood outside the inn, sweeping the same patch of steps.
>
> 'You're the one living up at Rook's place?'
>
> You nodded.
>
> She looked at you for a moment.
>
> 'Well. Somebody ought to.'
>
> She went back to sweeping.

**Production constraints:** 'nearly an hour' is provisional narrative travel language, **not** a promised in-game duration (current day is only 420 real seconds). The older woman is unspecified unless the owner approves a named resident and dialog. This is a tone reference only, not a canonical first visit or a mandated cutscene.

## Quality rubric for every major scene
Score in review (0–2 points each, total 12):
1. **Clarity:** Anyone can understand what literally happens.
2. **Specificity:** At least one memorable detail not replaceable by a generic fantasy phrase.
3. **Character:** The speaker's work, history or priorities color what they say.
4. **Subtext:** Emotional meaning emerges without a speech explaining it.
5. **Agency:** Player's prior choice or current behavior can matter without false moral grading.
6. **Restraint:** No lore dump, forced feeling, needless filler or prematurely solved mystery.

Aim for 10–12 in key scenes, but clarity and integrity override any aesthetic score. A scene with zero plot movement can be valuable as a breather; a scene that is only flowery but empty is not.

## Editorial process
- Define the **event and its consequence** in plain factual terms first.
- Decide who has something at stake; write each person as someone living a normal life, not as a quest dispenser.
- Draft the scene in ordinary spoken English.
- Remove redundant explanation and generic intensifiers.
- Add one grounded sensory/detail beat if it helps; avoid lists of four metaphors in every paragraph.
- Test distinct choice paths for honesty: each must reflect actual player action, not author favoritism.
- Read dialogue aloud for natural rhythm; screen-test short text lengths under current controller/diary UI.
- Verify all canonical names, dates, main-story flags, fog uncertainty and save-dependent scenes before production.

## Apply to first-year branching
- **Autumn — The Stranger:** scarcity, first encounter with Maren, wood, first crops; suspicion without overwriting existing first-sighting sequence.
- **Winter — The Keeper:** wet boots, a properly stoked hearth, an honest request for provisions, practical barter, no 'good person' scoring.
- **Spring — The Neighbor:** thaw, road downhill, first Wicker Cove encounter, working harbor life and divided attitudes about Rook's place.
- **Summer — The One Who Stays:** neighbors remember promises; village investment and homestead choices appear through changed routine, not repeated reputation exposition.
- **September — Looking back:** write only memories the save verifies; the diary never tells the player they achieved a feeling or revealed a mystery they did not.
